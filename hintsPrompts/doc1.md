 Places Under Benin (Bini) Rule


At its peak between the 15th and 17th centuries, the Benin Empire expanded far beyond its Edo-speaking heartland into southwestern and south-central Nigeria. Benin's rule was characterized by the collection of traditional royal tributes and the placement of royal representatives (Ogba).
• The Edo Heartland: Benin City (Edo), Esan land, and Etsako communities.
• Lagos (Eko): Founded as a Benin military outpost camp known as Eko under Oba Orhogbua. The Oba of Lagos's lineage historically traces directly to Benin.
• Eastern Yoruba Kingdoms: Akure, Owo, and parts of Ekiti land.
• Western Niger Delta: The Itsekiri kingdom (Warri), Urhobo territories, and Ijaw coastal communities.
• Western Igboland (Anioma): Towns along the west bank of the Niger River, such as Asaba, Agbor, Ogwashi-Ukwu, and Issele-Ukwu.
• Onitsha: Located on the eastern bank of the Niger River, Onitsha was founded by migrants fleeing the Benin Empire (the Umuezechima migration).


2. Places Under Oyo Rule


The Oyo Empire was a formidable Yoruba imperial power that dominated the savannah and forest zones of southwestern Nigeria and parts of the modern-day Benin Republic and Togo. Driven by its elite cavalry, Oyo reached its zenith in the 17th and 18th centuries.
• The Yoruba Heartland: Metropolitan Oyo (Oyo-Ile), Northern Yoruba towns, and parts of the Igbomina and Ekiti regions.
• Egbado (Yewa) and Awori Lands: Towns in modern Ogun State, such as Ilaro (founded by the Oyo warrior Aro), Imeko, and Badagry. Oyo aggressively colonized these areas to protect its direct trade corridors to the coast.
• Republic of Benin (Dahomey): The Kingdom of Dahomey was defeated by Oyo and forced to pay heavy annual tributes to the Alaafin of Oyo for over half a century.
• Porto-Novo (Ajase): A crucial coastal trading port heavily integrated into Oyo's commercial network.
• Sabe and Ketu: Western Yoruba kingdoms located along the border of modern Nigeria and the Benin Republic.
• Nupe and Borgu Borderlands: Southern pockets of Nupe (Tapa) and Bariba (Borgu) territories were occasionally forced into tributary status.


3. How Aro Was Founded & Its Early Founders


The founding of Arochukwu (the center of the later Aro Confederacy) in the late 17th century was the result of a multi-ethnic civil war known as the Aro-Ibibio Wars.

The Conflict

The area of Arochukwu was originally inhabited by the Proto-Ibibio people, who established early states like Obong Okon Ita and Ibom. Over time, Igbo immigrants (specifically the Eze Agwu clan) migrated into the region. Tensions over land and resources escalated into a bloody stalemate between the Ibibio and the Igbo.

The Alliance & Firepower

To break the deadlock, the Igbo leader, Nna Uru (of the Eze Agwu clan), invited Nnachi, a powerful doctor-priest from the nearby Edda clan. Nnachi brokered an alliance with the Akpa people from east of the Cross River. The Akpa possessed a massive military advantage: early European firearms.
Led by Akpa warlords, the allied forces launched a decisive campaign, defeated the Ibibio forces, and drove them out. The victorious factions integrated, creating a unified state composed of 19 villages.

The Early Founders

• Eze Agwu (and Nna Uru): Leaders of the original Igbo immigrant faction that initiated the campaign to claim the territory.
• Nnachi: The mystical Edda priest whose spiritual guidance and diplomacy unified the forces. His lineage formed the core intellectual and spiritual class of the state.
• Osim and Akuma Nnubi: The Akpa brothers and military commanders who brought the firearms. Osim was killed during the war, and in honor of his sacrifice, his brother Akuma was made the first Eze Aro (King of Arochukwu).
• Oke Nnachi: The son of Nnachi. Following Akuma's reign, the kingship returned to the Igbo lineage through Oke Nnachi, establishing the royal dynasty that endures to this day.




1. Core Imperial Territories (Direct Igala Rule)


These were the core territories of the kingdom, ruled directly by the Attah through regional kings (Onu) and traditional royal councils. Today, these make up the bulk of Kogi East:
• Idah: The imperial capital and seat of the Attah.
• Ankpa, Anyigba, and Dekina: Major strategic urban and administrative command centers.
• Other Core Districts: Ugwolawo, Ejule, Ajaka, Egume, Omala, Olamaboro, Ofu, and Ibaji.


2. Vassal States and Tributary Territories


The Igala Empire expanded through aggressive trade dominance, military garrisons, and political installations. Many neighboring mini-states and communities became vassals, paying regular royal tribute to Idah to secure safety from slave raids and ensure political legitimacy.

Northern Igboland (Nsukka and Anambra River Basin)

By the late 17th century, Igala conquered and established firm socio-economic, political, and religious control over several northern Igbo communities. Igala warlords like Omeppa set up military garrisons in these areas.
• Nsukka Borderlands: The autonomous towns of Opi, Nsukka, Nkpologu, Ibagwa-Ani, Akpugo, and Enugu-Ezike. Their local kings (Ezes) could only validate their authority by traveling to Idah for coronation, investiture, and to receive royal choral beads (aka) and staffs of office from the Attah.
• Anambra River Communities: Areas like Nsugbe and several riverine Igbo clans on the lower Niger.

Western Idoma Land

As established earlier, several western clans of the Idoma people fell under the direct tributary sphere of the Attah.
• Key Areas: Igumale, Otukpa, Ugboju, Onyagede, Agila, and Adoka. Local leadership required confirmation from the Idah court.

Mid-Niger and West Bank Communities

• Koton-Karfe: Located to the north of the confluence, this Egbura kingdom was under heavy political vassalage to the Attah.
• Etsako and Akoko-Edo Territories: Pockets of Etsako land (in modern northern Edo State), such as Agenebode, Illushi, and Anegbete, paid allegiance to or were influenced heavily by Igala border patrols.


3. External Igala Dynastic Branches & Colonies


Beyond structural vassals, the Igala kingdom expanded its footprint through "diaspora colonies" and dynastic founders who left Idah to establish rule over entirely different ethnic groups:
• The Nupe Kingdom Kingdom: Nupe tradition directly credits Tsoede (or Edgi), an Igala prince from Idah, with conquering and founding the unified Nupe Kingdom in the 16th century. He introduced advanced bronze casting and political structures from Idah.
• The Kingship of Onitsha: While Onitsha has deep roots tied to the Benin Umuezechima migration, Igala merchant groups and royals heavily influenced its early monarchical development, establishing permanent trading outposts.
• The Western Niger Delta (Delta Igala): Due to centuries of expansion and trade migration down the Niger River, several indigenous Igala communities emerged deep within Delta State. Towns like Ebu (which remains actively Igala-speaking), Illah, Oko, Amai, Ossissa, Onu-Aboh, and Obiaruku hold strong historical patterns of ethnogenesis or political links tracing back to Idah.
