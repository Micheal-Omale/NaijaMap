# The southern minorities: the Mid-West, the COR and Rivers movements, and the Cameroons

Research notes for HistoNaija, compiled 2026-10-29, as the southern companion to
[middle-belt.md](middle-belt.md). Nothing here is in the map data yet. Confidence marks
follow the project's rules: **high**, **medium**, **disputed**. Hausa and Fulani are named
as separate peoples throughout. The owner's view that the Anioma are a fusion of Igala,
Igbo, Benin and Olukumi peoples is kept.

---

## 1. The short answer

The North had the Middle Belt. The South had its own minority question, in two parts. In the
**Western Region** the Yoruba were about three-quarters of the people, and the Edo, Urhobo,
Itsekiri, Isoko, western Ijaw and Anioma of Benin and Delta provinces asked to be let out.
In the **Eastern Region** the Igbo were the largest people, and the Ibibio, Efik, Annang,
Ijaw, Ogoni, Ejagham and others of Calabar, Ogoja and Rivers provinces did the same.

- **The West's minorities won first.** The Mid-Western Region, created on 9 August 1963,
  was the only new region of the First Republic. It came after a decade of campaigning by
  the Oba of Benin, Otu-Edo and Dennis Osadebay's Mid-West State Movement. It became
  possible only when the Action Group, which had blocked it, was broken by its own crisis
  in 1962. On 13 July 1963, 579,077 people voted yes and 7,218 voted no.
- **The East's minorities waited until 1967.** The Calabar-Ogoja-Rivers (COR) state movement
  was born in 1953, after the NCNC forced out Eyo Ita, an Ibibio from Eket, as leader of the
  Eastern government. The Willink Commission refused it a state in 1958. Isaac Boro's
  "Niger Delta Republic" lasted twelve days in February 1966. Gowon's decree of 27 May 1967
  finally gave **Rivers** and **South-Eastern** states, three days before Biafra seceded.
- **The Cameroons.** British Cameroons was ruled as part of Nigeria from 1922 to 1960, in two
  pieces. **Southern Cameroons left.** Its members walked out of the Eastern House of
  Assembly in 1953, complaining of being dominated. It won its own government in 1954, and on
  11 February 1961 it voted by 233,571 to 97,741 to join the Republic of Cameroun. **Northern
  Cameroons did not leave.** In 1959 it voted to put off joining Nigeria, which the UN
  commissioner read as "a protest vote against the system of local administration". After
  reforms it voted on 11–12 February 1961, by 146,296 to 97,659, to join Nigeria, and on 1
  June 1961 it became Sardauna Province of Northern Nigeria. Cameroun took Britain to the
  International Court of Justice over the northern vote and lost in 1963.

**How the southern case differs from the Middle Belt's.** The Middle Belt defined itself
against the heritage of the Sokoto Caliphate, Islam and Hausa rule. The southern minorities
defined themselves against **majority-party regions**: the Yoruba Action Group in Ibadan and
the Igbo-led NCNC in Enugu. In time they also defined themselves around **oil**, which was
found at Oloibiri, an Ijaw town, in 1956. There was no caliphate in the South. The fear was of
being outvoted, out-hired and out-developed. In the East it was sharpened after 1967 by the
war, which was fought largely on minority land.

## 2. The three regions and their minorities, 1946–1967

- **The Richards Constitution (1946)** set up three regional assemblies: North, West and East.
  **Macpherson (1951)** gave them more power, and **Lyttelton (1954)** made Nigeria a federation
  of three regions plus Lagos. Each region came to be ruled by one party built on its largest
  people. *(high: wikipedia-richards-constitution; repo politics/self-government.json)*
- **The West** was about 75% Yoruba. Two of its eight provinces, Benin and Delta (called Warri
  until 1952), were mostly minority peoples, about a fifth of the region's population.
  *(high: wikipedia-western-region)*
- **The East's** largest peoples were the Igbo, Ibibio and Ijaw, Nigeria's third, fourth and
  fifth largest. Historians put the minorities at about 40% of what became Biafra's
  population, holding well over half its land. *(high for the ranking: wikipedia-eastern-
  region; medium for the 40%)*
- **The North**: see [middle-belt.md](middle-belt.md).

### State by state: who these peoples are

| **Delta** | West: Delta (Warri) Province | Urhobo, Isoko, Itsekiri, western Ijaw; Anioma (Aniocha, Oshimili), Ika, Ukwuani (Ndokwa) | Urhobo and Isoko Edoid; Itsekiri **Yoruboid**; Ijaw Ijoid; Anioma, Ika and Ukwuani Igboid | Mid-West; the Anioma at first hesitated |

*Language families: high. The Ebonyi-in-COR point is medium: Ogoja Province included Abakaliki
and Afikpo, but the exact 15 divisions of the COR memorandum were not seen.*

Two tests matter here, as they did in the North. Most southern minorities were **never ruled
by a Yoruba or Igbo state before 1900**. The South had no conquest state of that kind: the
Aro network was a trading and oracle system, not a government, and Benin, an Edo state, had
ruled parts of Yorubaland, not the other way round. Most of them also **do not speak the
majority language**. The exceptions are the Igboid-speaking Anioma, Ika, Ukwuani, Ikwerre,
Etche, Ekpeye and Ogba, and the Yoruboid-speaking Itsekiri. Those are exactly the peoples
whose place is still argued over today (section 8).

**Why it succeeded where the others failed.** The Mid-West was one compact block, with a
historic centre (Benin) and a ready-made party machine (the NCNC). Above all, it was carved
out of the region of the party that was then in **opposition** in Lagos. The NPC and NCNC
majority in the federal parliament had every reason to cut the Action Group's West down to
size. The Middle Belt and COR states would have been taken from the NPC's North and the
NCNC's East, the two parties in government, so neither was created. *(medium: this is the
standard reading, consistent with Omoigui's timeline)*

## 4. The East: Eyo Ita, COR, Rivers and Boro, 1953–1967

### The Eyo Ita crisis, 1953
- Eyo Ita, an Ibibio from Mkpanak in Eket and a founder of the Nigerian Youth Movement, was
  Leader of Government Business in the East. In January 1953 the NCNC ordered its ministers
  to resign. Ita and five others refused, and the NCNC drove him out so that Nnamdi Azikiwe
  could take the East.
- Udo Udoma (Ibibio) and Okoi Arikpo (Yakurr, of Ugep) left the NCNC in protest. With Ita
  they formed the National Independence Party, later the United National Independence Party
  (UNIP), which allied with the Action Group in the East.
  *(high: wikipedia-eyo-ita, wikipedia-udo-udoma, wikipedia-okoi-arikpo)*

### The COR state movement, 1953–1958
- Founded in 1953, with **Udo Udoma as president** and **Okoi Arikpo as secretary-general**.
  It asked for a Calabar-Ogoja-Rivers state of 15 administrative divisions, taken out of the
  East. Ita joined it, then went back to the NCNC.
  *(high: wikipedia-okoi-arikpo; medium for the 15 divisions)*
- **The Rivers split.** Harold Dappa-Biriye (Bonny) co-founded the Ijo Peoples League in 1941.
  In 1953 the Action Group backed a Rivers state, but the plan to merge it into COR with
  Calabar and Ogoja pushed him away. The **Council of Rivers Chiefs** (1953) became the
  Rivers Chiefs and Peoples' Congress (1954) and then the **Rivers Chiefs and Peoples'
  Conference** (1956). At the 1957 conference it argued for the status the Oil Rivers chiefs
  had held under their treaties of the 1880s. *(high: wikipedia-dappa-biriye,
  wikipedia-rivers-state)*

### Willink and the "special area", 1958
- **No COR state and no Rivers state.** Willink found that a new state in each region "would
  create problems as great as those it sought to cure". It recommended that **Calabar** be a
  "minority area" with a Calabar council, and that the **Niger Delta** be "a special area"
  with a board to supplement its development. It added fundamental rights in the
  constitution and "one strong police force" under federal control.
  *(high: dailytimes-1958-willink)*
- The **Niger Delta Development Board** followed (provided for in the 1960 constitution). It
  is the ancestor of today's NDDC. *(high)*

### Isaac Boro's twelve days, February 1966
- Isaac Adaka Boro, an Ijaw from Kaiama, was born at Oloibiri and was a former policeman and
  student union president at Nsukka. With Sam Owonaro and Nottingham Dick he trained the
  **Niger Delta Volunteer Force** behind his father's compound at Kaiama. On **23 February
  1966**, weeks after the January coup, he proclaimed a **Niger Delta Republic**.
- It held out for twelve days, then Boro was captured by soldiers under Ojukwu, the East's
  military governor, and tried for treason. Gowon pardoned him in May 1967. He joined the
  federal army as a major and was killed near Okrika on 9 May 1968. Saro-Wiwa, Asari Dokubo
  and the Ijaw youth movements later claimed him.
  *(high: wikipedia-isaac-adaka-boro, wikipedia-rivers-state)*

### 1967: states, secession, and war on minority land
- **27 May 1967.** Gowon's Decree No. 14 split the East into **East-Central State** (the Igbo
  heartland, capital Enugu), **Rivers State** (Port Harcourt, under Alfred Diete-Spiff, a
  25-year-old Ijaw naval officer) and **South-Eastern State** (Calabar). Biafra seceded three
  days later, claiming the whole East. *(high)*
- **The minorities were divided.** Some backed Biafra, many did not, and some were forced.
  Minorities suffered at the hands of both sides. Federal troops took Calabar in October 1967
  and Port Harcourt on 19 May 1968, so much of the war was fought on minority land. Okoi
  Arikpo, the COR secretary of 1953, served as Gowon's foreign minister (1967–75) and worked
  to deny Biafra recognition. *(high for the dates and Arikpo; medium for the divided
  loyalties: secondary summaries)*

### After the war
- **"Abandoned property."** Rivers State edicts (1969; Edict No. 10 of 1970), later Decree No.
  90 of 1979, let the state take over and sell Igbo-owned houses in Port Harcourt as
  "abandoned". Igbo owners and writers call it expropriation. Obasanjo's account says the
  state collected rent for absent owners, at a time when local people had lost their own
  homes. In 2004 the Supreme Court found that in one case the procedure had not been
  followed. The dispute is still alive. *(disputed: both accounts kept)*
- **Later states.** South-Eastern was renamed Cross River in 1976. **Akwa Ibom** (the Ibibio
  and Annang) was carved out in 1987 after a campaign begun in 1980. **Bayelsa** (the Ijaw
  heartland) came out of Rivers in 1996, and **Ebonyi** (the old Ogoja Igbo divisions) in
  1996. The **South-South** zone of six states (Akwa Ibom, Bayelsa, Cross River, Delta, Edo,
  Rivers) followed Alex Ekwueme's panel under Abacha. *(high)*
- **Oil and the new minority politics.** These include the Ogoni struggle under Ken Saro-Wiwa,
  executed in 1995, the Ijaw Youth Council's **Kaiama Declaration** (1998), MEND, and the
  NDDC (2000). The Willink grievance became a resource-control grievance.
  *(high: wikipedia-niger-delta-volunteer-force; Saro-Wiwa well attested)*

## 5. The Cameroons: why the South left and the North stayed

### Background
- Germany's Kamerun was split in 1916–19. Britain took two strips along Nigeria's border,
  **Northern Cameroons** (itself in two separate pieces) and **Southern Cameroons**, as a
  League of Nations mandate (1922). They became a UN trust territory in 1946. Both were run
  *from* Nigeria but were not legally part of it. The north was administered with Northern
  Nigeria (Adamawa, Dikwa and Benue). The south was ruled from Enugu with the Eastern
  Region. *(high: wikipedia-british-cameroons, wikipedia-southern-cameroons)*

### Why Southern Cameroons left
1. **Feeling dominated in the East.** Thirteen Southern Cameroonians sat in the Eastern House
   at Enugu from 1951. In 1953, "unhappy with the domineering attitude of Nigerian
   politicians and lack of unity among the ethnic groups in the Eastern Region", they
   declared **"benevolent neutrality"** in the Eyo Ita crisis and walked out. At the London
   conference of July–August 1953 they asked for a region of their own. On 1 October 1954
   Southern Cameroons became an autonomous region with its own House at Buea under
   E. M. L. Endeley. *(high: wikipedia-southern-cameroons, wikipedia-endeley)*
2. **Feeling against Nigerians, above all the Igbo.** Igbo traders and workers were prominent
   in Southern Cameroons' towns and plantations. Joseph Nfi lists among the realities of the
   debate "a deep-seated antipathy towards Nigerians, especially the Igbo people, felt by much
   of the Southern Cameroon population". Pro-Ambazonian writers call it "Ibo domination and
   hegemony". *(high for Nfi's finding: nfi-2011; the stronger wording is partisan)*
3. **The pull of reunification.** Many had kin across the border in French Cameroun. John
   Ngu Foncha's **KNDP** (1955) worked the countryside on "bread-and-butter issues" and
   ethnic feeling. It beat Endeley's pro-Nigeria KNC in 1959 (14 seats to 12).
   *(high: nfi-2011, wikipedia-kndp)*
4. **No independence option.** The ballot offered only Nigeria or Cameroun. Independence
   alone was ruled out at the UN, opposed by Britain's Andrew Cohen and by African delegations.
   *(high: wikipedia-1961-referendum)*
5. **Fear of insecurity worked the other way.** The pro-Nigeria camp warned of the UPC
   insurgency next door: about 5,000 fighters crossed over, and Balewa broadcast on security.
   Nfi thinks the threat was "more apparent than real", exaggerated by the British.
   *(medium: nfi-2011)*

**Result, 11 February 1961:** turnout 94.75%; **233,571 for Cameroun (70.5%), 97,741 for
Nigeria.** Southern Cameroons joined on 1 October 1961 as West Cameroon in a federation. The
federation was abolished in 1972, and that grievance runs on into today's Anglophone crisis
and the Ambazonia movement. *(high: un-repertory-art76, wikipedia-southern-cameroons)*

### Why Northern Cameroons first said "not yet", then "Nigeria"
- **7 November 1959.** Asked whether to join Northern Nigeria or decide later, almost 88%
  voted, and **62% chose to decide later**. The UN commissioner judged that most voters had
  used the plebiscite "to register what was, in effect, a protest vote against the system of
  local administration prevailing in the Northern Cameroons". That system was rule through
  the emirate-style Native Authorities of Adamawa and Dikwa. *(high: un-repertory-art76)*
- **The reforms that followed.** The UN General Assembly (resolution 1473, December 1959)
  called for "further decentralization" and "effective democratization of the system of
  local government", and for the territory's administration to be separated from Nigeria's by
  1 October 1960. The British made it a province, removed a number of unpopular authorities
  and brought in more local representation. *(high for the UN text; medium for the reforms:
  ARJ article seen via summary)*
- **11–12 February 1961.** With universal adult suffrage, unlike 1959, of 292,985 registered,
  **146,296 voted to join Nigeria and 97,659 to join Cameroun (60%).** Commentators note that
  the north was largely Muslim and tied to Yola and Maiduguri, and that some voters did not
  realise the links to Northern Nigeria had been kept at provincial level. *(high for the
  figures; medium for the reasons)*
- **1 June 1961.** It became **Sardauna Province** of Northern Nigeria. Its land is now in
  Borno (around Gwoza), Adamawa (Mubi, Michika, Madagali) and Taraba (the Mambilla, Sardauna
  LGA). *(high)*
- **Cameroun's challenge.** Cameroun voted against the UN endorsement (resolution 1608, passed
  62 to 23 with 10 abstentions), alleging irregularities and pro-Nigerian interference, and
  sued Britain on 30 May 1961. On 2 December 1963 the International Court of Justice declined
  to rule, since nothing it said could undo the result. *(high: icj-northern-cameroons,
  un-repertory-art76)*

*A later echo:* the ICJ gave the Bakassi peninsula, home to Efik and Ibibio fishing towns, to
Cameroon in 2002, and Nigeria handed it over in 2008. *(high; not researched further here)*

## 6. The Willink Commission in the South

Willink sat from 23 November 1957 to 12 June 1958 and reported on 30 July 1958.

| Demand | Willink's answer |
|---|---|
| Mid-West state (Edo, Urhobo, Itsekiri, Anioma) | No state. The Edo districts become a "minority area" with a council for Edo affairs. |
| COR state (Calabar, Ogoja, Rivers) | No state. Calabar becomes a "minority area" with a Calabar council. |
| Rivers / Ijaw state | No. A separate Ijaw state was rejected. The Niger Delta becomes a "special area" with a development board. |
| All regions | Fundamental rights in the constitution; one federal police force; no change to the North–West boundary without a plebiscite. |

The general reason: new states "would create problems as great as those it sought to
cure", meaning new minorities inside them, and doubts about whether they could pay their way.
*(high: dailytimes-1958-willink; omoigui-midwest)*

## 7. How the southern case compares with the Middle Belt's

| | Middle Belt | Southern minorities |
|---|---|---|
| Against whom | The caliphate heritage, emirate rule, Islam and Hausa as a ruling identity ("Arewa") | The majority-party regions: the Yoruba AG in the West, the Igbo-led NCNC in the East |
| Precolonial experience | Raids, tribute and resistance on the jihad frontier | No conquest by a Yoruba or Igbo state; Benin and the delta states were powers of their own |
| Colonial grievance | Ruled through emirs and Hausa agents | Outvoted in regional assemblies; Southern Cameroons "dominated" at Enugu |
| Movements | Non-Muslim League → UMBC (Tarka) | BDPP, Otu-Edo, Mid-West State Movement (Osadebay); COR (Udoma, Arikpo); Rivers Chiefs (Dappa-Biriye); KNC/KNDP in the Cameroons |
| Willink, 1958 | No state | No state; Edo and Calabar "minority areas"; the Delta a "special area" |
| First win | Benue-Plateau, 1967 | Mid-West, 1963; Rivers and South-Eastern, 1967 |
| What came after | Land, chieftaincy, sharia, herders | Oil, resource control, Ogoni, the Kaiama Declaration |

Both were "won" in the end by the same man, Gowon, an Ngas from the Middle Belt, on the same
day: 27 May 1967.

## 8. Where the southern case is strong, and where it is complicated

### Strong
- **The Edo, Urhobo, Isoko, Ijaw, Ibibio, Annang, Efik, Ejagham and Ogoni** are clearly not
  Yoruba and not Igbo in language, history or self-identification.
- **The Mid-West vote** (579,077 to 7,218) is about as clear a statement of a minority's will
  as any in Nigeria's history.
- **Southern Cameroons** chose to leave Nigeria in a UN-supervised vote.

### Complicated
- **The Igboid-speaking peoples**: the Anioma, Ika and Ukwuani in Delta, and the Ikwerre,
  Etche, Ekpeye and Ogba in Rivers. Linguists put their languages in the Igboid family. Many
  of their people and organisations say they are not Igbo; others, and pan-Igbo bodies, say
  they are. In January 2025 the election of an Ikwerre man, John Azuta-Mbata, as
  president-general of Ohanaeze Ndigbo was rejected by a Rivers group on exactly this ground.
  The owner's view is that the Anioma are a fusion of Igala, Igbo, Benin and Olukumi
  peoples. The repo's group records already mark these debates as disputed.
  *(disputed throughout)*
- **The Itsekiri** speak a Yoruboid language, yet joined the Mid-West, though divided: the Olu
  and O. N. Rewane opposed it, while Festus Okotie-Eboh supported it.
- **COR claimed Igbo areas too.** Ogoja Province included Abakaliki and Afikpo, which shows
  that "minority" maps were also political claims.
- **The minorities fought each other.** In the Warri crisis (1997–2003) the Itsekiri fought the
  Ijaw and later the Urhobo over local government headquarters and oil money, and over
  200,000 people were displaced.
- **Akenzua served in the AG government** (1955–60) while backing the Mid-West, a reminder that
  minority leaders bargained with the majorities as well as against them.
- **The Biafra years cut both ways.** Minorities suffered from both sides. The post-war
  "abandoned property" takeovers are remembered by Igbo owners as dispossession and by some
  minority residents as justice.
