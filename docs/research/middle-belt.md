# The Middle Belt: its peoples, the history that matters, and why they are not "Arewa"

Research notes for HistoNaija, compiled 2026-10-29. Nothing here is in the map data yet.
Confidence marks follow the project's rules: **high**, **medium**, **disputed**.
House style: Hausa and Fulani are named as separate peoples, and "Hausa-Fulani" is not used
in our own voice. Where a source uses it, the point is paraphrased.

---

## 1. The short answer

"Arewa" is the Hausa word for *the north*. As a political identity it means the heritage of
the Sokoto Caliphate and Kanem-Bornu, carried into the colonial Northern Region and into the
Northern People's Congress (which began as *Jam'iyyar Mutanen Arewa*, "the Association of
the People of the North") under the slogan "One North, One People". The Middle Belt case
against being called Arewa rests on seven points. Each one is backed in the sections below.

1. **They are older than that story and stood outside it.** Nok (central Nigeria, from about
   1500 BCE) is older than any Hausa state by some two thousand years. Kwararafa, the
   confederacy of the Benue valley, sacked Kano and besieged the Bornu capital in the 1600s.
   The Middle Belt was a rival centre of power, not the edge of someone else's.
2. **Most of them were never ruled by Sokoto or Bornu.** The Plateau peoples, the Tiv, the
   Jukun of Wukari, the Igala, the Idoma heartland, the Ebira hills, Koton Karfe, the
   Bachama, the Tangale hills and most Southern Kaduna peoples fought the jihad off, hid in
   the hills, or bought peace with tribute under truces (*amana*), without being absorbed.
   For the emirates they were the frontier, a land to raid for slaves.
3. **They governed themselves differently.** Most were run by elders, clan heads and
   priest-kings: the Aku of the Jukun, the Attah of Igala, the Agwom of the Afizere, the Kwe
   Kukwen of the Kuteb. These were not emirates, and many had no single ruler at all.
4. **The British put them under emirs and Hausa agents, and they rebelled.** Colonial
   "indirect rule" placed Southern Zaria under Zaria, the Plateau under Bauchi, and Tiv and
   Idoma districts under imported Muslim, Hausa-speaking chiefs, clerks and tax collectors.
   Revolts followed: the Atyap in 1910 and 1922, the Idoma from 1899 to 1914, the Igede in
   1926–29, and the Makurdi riots in 1947. This is where Middle Belt consciousness was made.
5. **Their religious path was different.** Traditional religions held on longest here.
   Christian missions, barred from the emirates, worked here instead, so Christianity became
   a marker of difference. But Islam is native here too: the Ebira, Nupe, Alago and others
   have large Muslim communities.
6. **They organised politically against "One North".** The Non-Muslim League (1949), the
   Middle Zone League (1950) and the United Middle Belt Congress (1955) asked for a Middle
   Belt region. The Willink Commission refused in 1958. The Tiv rose in 1960 and 1964. A
   Middle Belt head of state, Yakubu Gowon, dissolved the Northern Region in 1967.
7. **Today they name it themselves.** The Middle Belt Forum defines its people as "the
   indigenous ethnic nationalities of Northern Nigeria that existed outside the authority of
   the Sokoto Caliphate and the Kanem-Borno Empire" before colonial rule. It says it seeks
   freedom from an "imposed Arewa identity", not separation from Nigeria.

Three processes sit underneath all seven, and each has its own section. **Hausanization**
(section 7) was the push, colonial above all, to make Middle Belt peoples Hausa in language,
names, dress, religion and rule. **Northernization and the Sardauna's "One North"** (section
8) put northerners first in jobs, which Middle Belters welcomed, but also brought conversion
campaigns and repression, which they resisted. **State creation** (section 10) was the
remedy they won: from one Northern Region in 1966 to Benue-Plateau and Kwara in 1967, and
on to Benue, Plateau, Kogi, Taraba and Nasarawa. Section 11 pulls the reasons together:
why the Arewa idea was rejected, and what was *not* rejected.

**The Middle Belt is not one block.** Section 2 goes state by state. **Benue and most of
Kogi are the clearest case.** They were never under Sokoto, and Hausa is not their language:
they speak Tiv, Idoma, Igede, Igala, Ebira, Okun Yoruba and English. Across the Plateau,
Nasarawa, Niger, the FCT and Southern Kaduna, by contrast, Hausa is the everyday second
language even where the caliphate never ruled. And Niger, Kwara and Nasarawa contain real
caliphate emirates.

The honest counterweight is in section 14. Emirates did exist inside the Middle Belt (Keffi,
Lafia, Nasarawa, Jema'a, Wase, Muri, Bida, Ilorin, Kontagora). Millions of people here are
Muslim and do identify with the North. And the Middle Belt label itself is contested, even by
some of the people it claims.

## 2. State by state: was it under Sokoto, and does it speak Hausa?

People often run two separate questions together, and the answers differ sharply from state
to state:

- **A. Was the area ruled by the Sokoto Caliphate (through an emirate) or by Bornu before
  1900?**
- **B. Is Hausa spoken there today as a home or everyday language?**

Being in the colonial **Northern Region** (1900–1967) is a third, different thing. Every
Middle Belt state was in it. Very few were in the caliphate. The British drew the line of
the Northern Protectorate in 1900, and that line, not Sokoto, first made Benue and Kogi
"northern".

*Confidence:* column A is **high** throughout. The sources are the repo's polity and society
records, Ochonu (2008), and the Wikipedia articles on the emirates and states. The two language columns
are **medium**. There has been no language census since 1963, so they rest on:
- Radio Benue's languages, and Tiv being taught in schools;
- Adegbija (2004) on Kogi's three main languages;
- Kperogi (2020) on the Okun;
- Wikipedia's note that Hausa "has replaced many other languages especially in the
  north-central and north-eastern part of Nigeria";
- Arasanyin (1995).

### What this shows: two tests, three groups
1. **Not under Sokoto and not Hausa-speaking.** Benue (Tiv, Idoma, Igede), and the Igala,
   Ebira and Bassa of Kogi. This is the strongest "not Arewa" case. They became "northern"
   only when the British drew the protectorate line in 1900, and their old links ran south
   and east as much as north: the Igala down the Niger to Onitsha and the delta; the Idoma
   and Igede to the Igbo and Cross River peoples; the Okun to Ekiti. Hausanization (section
   7) barely touched their languages. What reached them was colonial rule through Hausa
   agents, which they fought (section 6).
2. **Not under Sokoto, but Hausa-speaking as a second language.** The Plateau peoples,
   Southern Kaduna, the hill peoples of Taraba, Adamawa, Gombe and Kebbi, and the Gbagyi and
   Koro of the FCT. Their case rests on history and identity, not on language. They speak
   Hausa without being Hausa.
3. **Under the caliphate.** Niger (Bida, Kontagora), Kwara (Ilorin), the Nasarawa emirate
   towns, and the southern Bauchi and Gombe plains. Many people here, above all the
   Muslims, do identify with the North. Even so, the **Okun of Kogi** were under Bida and
   still speak Yoruba, not Hausa.

## 3. What "Middle Belt" means

- **The term.** It began in linguistics, for the belt of what were then called "semi-Bantu"
  languages (Struck; Northcote Thomas, 1913). Forde and Scott used it geographically for
  Nigeria in 1946. It has no official status; the constitution's zone is "North Central".
  *(high: wikipedia-middle-belt)*
- **The area usually meant.** Plateau, Nasarawa, Benue, Kogi, Kwara, Niger, the FCT,
  Adamawa and Taraba (old Gongola), plus Southern Kaduna, southern Bauchi, southern Gombe,
  southern Kebbi (Zuru), southern Borno (the Bura, Chibok and Gwoza hills) and southern
  Yobe. *(high: wikipedia-middle-belt)*
- **Two definitions in tension.**
  - *Geographic:* the southern half of the old Northern Region, a zone between the Muslim far
    north and the Christian south, with no majority people and over 230 languages.
  - *Historical-political* (the Middle Belt Forum): the peoples who were outside Sokoto and
    Kanem-Bornu before colonial rule. On this definition the Bura of southern Borno count and
    the Muslim emirate towns of Kwara and Niger may not. *(high: leadership-pogu-bitrus)*
  - *The critics' reading* (Farooq Kperogi, 2020): in practice "Middle Belt" means "northern
    Nigerian Christians who are not ethnically Hausa". He points out that leading voices
    such as Bala Takaya and Dan Suleiman are from Adamawa, outside the North Central zone.
    *(high: kperogi-2020)*
- **Population and religion.** No ethnic census since 1963. One rough estimate, uncertain,
  is 45% Christian, 45% Muslim and 10% traditional religion. *(medium: wikipedia-middle-belt)*

### Languages: not the dividing line

The Middle Belt holds three of Africa's four language families:

| Family | Middle Belt examples |
|---|---|
| Niger–Congo: Benue–Congo | Tiv (Tivoid); Jukun, Kuteb (Jukunoid); Idoma, Alago, Igede (Idomoid); Berom, Atyap, Bajju, Ham, Eggon, Mada, Afizere, Irigwe, Tarok (Plateau); Gbagyi, Koro, Kambari, Dakarkari (Kainji, Nupoid); Nupe, Ebira (Nupoid); Igala and Okun Yoruba (Yoruboid) |
| Niger–Congo: Adamawa | Mumuye, Chamba Leko, Waja, Longuda |
| Afro-Asiatic: Chadic | Ngas, Mwaghavul, Goemai, Tangale, Bura, Kamwe, Kilba, Bachama and Bata, Sayawa (Zaar), and **Gwandara, the closest relative of Hausa** |
| Nilo-Saharan | the Kanuri ruling houses of Lafia |

The point is that many Middle Belt languages are Chadic, the same family as Hausa, yet those
peoples are not Arewa. The Gwandara speak Hausa's nearest sister language, and their own
tradition says they left Kano rather than accept Islam. **The line between the Middle Belt and
Arewa is political history, not language.** *(high: wikipedia-gwandara-language;
disputed for the Gwandara exodus: see section 13)*

## 4. Deep time: before the Hausa states and the caliphate

### Nok (c. 1500 BCE to the early centuries CE)
- Named after the Ham village of Nok in Southern Kaduna. The first terracotta came out of a
  tin mine there in 1928; Bernard Fagg recognised the culture from 1943.
  *(high: wikipedia-nok)*
- Farming settlements from about 1500 BCE. The terracottas are dated from about 900 BCE, and
  radiocarbon and thermoluminescence tests put them at roughly 2,900 to 2,000 years old. They
  are the oldest large figurative sculpture in Africa outside Egypt. Late pieces from Chado,
  near Godogodo, date to about the 6th century CE. *(high: wikipedia-nok,
  wikipedia-southern-kaduna)*
- Nok iron-working and art run across Southern Kaduna, the Plateau edge and Nasarawa. That is
  Middle Belt country, more than a thousand years before Kano's first king in the Kano
  Chronicle.

### Kwararafa (c. 1000s to 1700s)
- A confederacy of the Benue valley led by the Jukun, with a divine king, the Aku, and wealth
  from the salt of Keana, Awe and Arufu. Idoma, Igala, Alago, Goemai and others marched in its
  armies or trace their kings to it. *(high: repo polities/kwararafa.json,
  wikipedia-jukun-people)*
- **It sat over the Hausa states, not under them.** The Kano Chronicle records Kwararafa
  invading Kano under Muhammad Zaki, so that Kano's people fled to Daura, and again in 1653,
  when it destroyed the Kofan Kawayi gate. The Katsina records give a Kwararafa invasion
  between 1670 and 1684. It tried to take Ngazargamu, the Bornu capital, under Mai Ali
  (1645–1684). Sultan Muhammad Bello of Sokoto later counted Kwararafa among the seven
  greatest kingdoms of the Sudan. *(high: wikipedia-jukun-people)*
- Webster's reading (via the Southern Kaduna article) is that Kwararafa became a refuge for
  people fleeing Islam in the north. *(medium)*
- Earlier, Kano took tribute of 200 slaves from Kwararafa under Kanajeji, and Zazzau under
  Amina claimed tribute from Nupe and Kwararafa. The relationship went both ways, but it was
  a relationship between rival powers. *(high)*

### Other old polities of the belt
- **Igala Kingdom** at Idah under the Attah. It fought Benin in 1515–16, and its reach ran
  from the Niger delta to Koton Karfe. *(high: repo polities/igala.json)*
- **Nupe**, united by Tsoede (Edegi) in the early 16th century. Tradition makes him the son
  of an Attah of Igala. Nupe sacked Oyo-Ile around 1535. *(high: repo polities/nupe.json)*
- **Borgu** (Bussa, Nikki, Illo). Its kings trace descent from Kisra, a legend that sets
  Borgu apart from its Muslim neighbours. It withstood Songhai and Nupe, sheltered Oyo's
  exiled kings, and its army died alongside Oyo's at Ilorin in 1837.
  *(high: repo polities/borgu.json)*
- **Biu** (Bura and Pabir), from Yamta-ra-Wala, about 1535. Mari Watirwa (1793–1838) beat
  back the Fulani from Gombe. *(high: repo societies/biu.json)*
- **Wukari**, the Jukun kingdom that carried Kwararafa's name, and the Aku Uka.
  *(high: wikipedia-wukari-federation)*

## 5. The jihad frontier, 1804–1900

### How the Middle Belt met the caliphate
- After 1804 the jihad overthrew the Hausa kings. New emirates on the southern frontier
  (Zaria, Bauchi, Muri, Adamawa, Bida, Ilorin, Keffi, Nasarawa, Lafia, Jema'a, Wase, Lapai,
  Kontagora) pushed into the Middle Belt. *(high)*
- Moses Ochonu sums the result up as "a series of complex stalemates, fluid accommodations,
  and tense, frequently violated treaties of co-existence". Nengel calls this the *amana*
  system: tribute paid for peace, which suited the emirates too, because wars were costly.
  Matthew Kukah: "Around the Middle Belt, the Jihadists seemed more preoccupied with
  slavery, economic and political expansionism than the spread of [Islam]."
  *(high: ochonu-2008)*
- In caliphate terms, the non-Muslim lands were *dar al-harb*, the "abode of war", where
  slave raiding was lawful. *(high: ochonu-2008)*

### Eyewitnesses to the raiding
- **Barth**, after Vogel's journey of 1855, on the emir of Bauchi: he "occupied with an
  imposing force the fields in the valley, driving all his horses in the then green harvest
  until the poor devils on the mountains surrendered for fear of starvation and sent down
  the number of boys requested by him, so he got in three weeks 200 fine slaves, which were
  marched off immediately to Sokoto for sale." *(high: wikipedia-southern-kaduna, quoting
  Barth)*
- **Baikie**, 1862, on the country between Bida and Kano: "a country devastated by war".
- **Rohlfs**, 1866: "the armed heathens do not distinguish the Christian from the Muslim,
  they know only the latter, who steal their children and women and carry them into
  slavery. And when they so revenge themselves, who can blame them?"
- In Ham villages, oral history says families pierced their daughters' upper lips so that
  slavers would value them less (Gadzama 2014). *(medium)*

### Resistance did not only come from non-Muslims
- **Ningi** (c. 1846–1902): a Kano Qur'anic scholar, Malam Hamza, broke with the emir and set
  up in the hills among the Butawa and Warjawa. From there they held off Bauchi, Zaria and
  Kano for half a century (Adell Patton 1975). *(high)*
- **Abuja (today's Suleja)**: the Hausa kings of Zazzau, driven out by the jihad, took refuge
  among the Koro and Gbagyi. Abu Ja founded Abuja in 1828. The British took it in 1902.
  This was Hausa resistance to the caliphate, inside the Middle Belt. *(high)*
- **The Gwandara**: by their own tradition, Hausa of Kano who left rather than accept Islam.
  *(disputed)*

### A caution on depopulation
Michael Mason (1969) argued that slave raiding does not explain the Middle Belt's low
population density everywhere. Some places gained people as emirate power settled down,
while some very thinly peopled areas were never raided at all. Gleave and Prothero (1971)
disagreed. So say that raiding devastated **particular** areas (Kontagora's country, the
Southern Zaria corridor, the Bauchi plateau edge), not that it emptied the whole belt.
*(high: mason-1969)*

## 6. Colonial rule, 1900–1960: "colonialism by proxy"

### The two administrations
- The British ran a two-track system: **emirate administration** and what they called
  **"pagan administration"**. In the second, the goal was to "prepare" Middle Belt peoples
  for indirect rule by placing emirate structures and Muslim, Hausa-speaking agents over
  them. Ochonu calls it "a civilizing mission within the civilizing mission", with "two sets
  of colonials—the British and the Hausa". *(high: ochonu-2008; book: ochonu-2014)*
- The agents were not only chiefs. They were "interpreters, traders, Muslim scholars,
  clerks, messengers, policemen, bodyguards, and 'political agents'". Many were not Hausa by
  birth but Nupe, Ilorin Yoruba or Gbagyi Muslims who "performed Hausaness" to get
  colonial jobs. *(high: ochonu-2008)*
- Lugard himself (1904) wrote of "the great pagan tribes of Central Nigeria", brave in war,
  speaking diverse languages, "never conquered by the Fulani". Then he put them under emirs.
  *(high: wikipedia-southern-kaduna)*

### Where it was done, and how people answered

**Southern Zaria (Southern Kaduna)**
- After 1907 it was run as if it were part of Zaria emirate, which it had never been. District
  heads and Native Authority officials were "entirely Hausa, Fulani, or Muslim", and only
  village heads were local. Zangon Katab District (1912) was ruled by Hausa officials from
  Zaria. *(high)*
- The Atyap rose against forced labour in 1910. The Atyap and Bajju refused tax in 1922.
- In 1925 the Resident admitted that non-Muslims avoided the Muslim *alkali* courts. The
  "pagan courts" set up in response ended up presided over by the Hausa district head.
- In 1934 Moroa, Jaba and Kagoro were moved into Zaria Province with indigenous, non-Muslim
  district heads, the only ones in the province. "Such independent districts were more
  peaceful." Yohanna Simon calls the system "Anglo/Hausa/Fulani rule".
- The first indigenous district head of Zangon Kataf came only in 1967. The Atyap chiefdom
  followed in 1995, and over ten more Southern Kaduna chiefdoms in 2001.
  *(high: wikipedia-southern-kaduna, wikipedia-atyap)*

**The Plateau**
- Ruled from Bauchi Province from 1902, "which had no earlier common history or control over
  it", until Plateau Province was created in 1926. The Plateau got direct rule, since it had
  never been under an emirate. *(high: wikipedia-plateau-state, wikipedia-jos)*
- Tin mining began in 1902. Jos grew as a mining town, drawing Hausa, Igbo and Yoruba
  migrants, which is the root of today's indigene-versus-settler disputes over Jos. The paramount stool of
  the Gbong Gwom Jos was created in 1935. *(high)*

**Tivland**
- The British conquest of 1906 was triggered when Tiv and Jukun fighters destroyed the Royal
  Niger Company station at Abinsi. They were not attacking British traders: they targeted
  the hundreds of Hausa traders and auxiliaries there. About 76 were killed and about 113
  taken captive. The victory declaration to the Tiv was written in Hausa and read by two
  Hausa interpreters. *(high: ochonu-2008)*
- In 1914 **Audu Dan Afoda**, a Nupe, Hausa-speaking Muslim, became Sarkin Makurdi. From 1914
  to 1926 the surrounding Tiv districts were put under him, and in 1927 he was made head of
  the Tiv council. In 1937 the Resident still called his rule a needed "educational
  influence". *(high: ochonu-2008, wikipedia-tor-tiv)*
- After he died in 1945, the British tried to appoint another "Hausa", and the **Makurdi
  riots of 1947** followed. The Tor Tiv office was created in 1946 under pressure from Tiv
  ex-servicemen such as Makir Zakpe, who became the first Tor Tiv.
  *(high: ochonu-2008, wikipedia-tor-tiv)*

**Idoma and Igede**
- In 1899 Odugbeho in Agatu was burned after its people killed a Hausa trader. More
  punitive raids followed: Aku in 1906, and 23 Adoka villages razed in 1907 after eight Hausa
  rubber collectors were killed. *(high: ochonu-2008)*
- In 1907 Alabi (Abubakar), a Muslim Yoruba trader from Ilorin, was made district head of
  Ugboju, with an Idoma, Amanyi, as his apprentice. Ugboju rose in 1910, Onyangede in 1912,
  and in 1914 the Ugboju people attacked the Hausa traders at Ombi. *(high: ochonu-2008)*
- In Igede, the "Hausa" clerks lived apart in their own quarters, built by Idoma labour. The
  **Ogbuloko uprising of 1926–29** was crushed by a large military operation. *(high)*
- The Idoma call Hausa people *Abakpa*. "As far as the Idoma were concerned any black man
  who came with the British and spoke to the white man in any language which was Hausa...
  straight away became Hausa" (Anyebe, via Ochonu). *(high)*
- The Och'Idoma was elected in 1947 and installed in 1949. *(high: repo thrones)*

### Missions and the "Great Prohibition"
- The British promised the emirs not to interfere with Islam, so missions were kept out of
  the Muslim emirates and went to the "pagan" areas instead (Barnes, "The Great
  Prohibition", 2010). *(high)*
- The Sudan United Mission (Karl Kumm, 1904; first station at Wase), the Sudan Interior
  Mission (today's ECWA), the CMS (Gowon's parents were CMS missionaries at Wusasa, Zaria),
  the Dutch Reformed mission among the Tiv (NKST), and the Catholic missions. A mission
  opened at Wukari in 1906. *(medium on exact dates, except Wukari 1906 high)*
- The result was mission schools and literacy in English. By 2025 Southern Kaduna alone
  counted "no less than 200 professors" who came through mission education (Kukah).
  Christianity became the marker of the Middle Belt. Andrew Barnes (2007): the movement was
  "the quest for asserting an esteemed Christian identity by non-Muslim northerners".
  *(high)*

## 7. Hausanization: making the Middle Belt "Hausa"

"Hausanization" here means the spread of the Hausa language, names, dress, religion and
style of rule among non-Hausa peoples, until they are seen, or come to see themselves, as
Hausa. Part of it was voluntary and old: trade and Islam. Part of it was colonial policy.
Middle Belt identity grew largely as a refusal of the second part.

### Before colonial rule: an assimilating identity
- Hausa became the lingua franca of the jihad states and was adopted "for political
  expediency" by the new emirates. Enwerem notes that Nigerians wrongly treat Hausa as if
  it meant "the north". *(high: enwerem-1995 ch. 1)*
- "Hausa" works as an open category. Mahdi Adamu: "the Hausa ethnic unit... is an
  assimilating ethnic entity and the Hausa language a colonizing one" (quoted in Ochonu).
  Muslim Nupe, Ilorin Yoruba and Gbagyi could "perform Hausaness" and be counted as Hausa.
  *(high: ochonu-2008)*
- In Hausa usage, the non-Muslim peoples were *arna* ("pagans"), and many still carry Hausa
  names that they did not choose:

| Hausa or colonial name | Own name |
|---|---|
| Munshi, Mitshi | Tiv |
| Gwari | Gbagyi |
| Kataf | Atyap |
| Kaje | Bajju |
| Jaba | Ham |
| Kagoro | Agworok |
| Kadara | Adara |
| Moroa | Asholio |
| Angas | Ngas |
| Jarawa, Jere | Afizere |
| Higi | Kamwe |
| Madan Dutse ("hill Mada") | Eggon |

  The move back to their own names (Atyap, Bajju, Ham, Kamwe, Gbagyi) is itself part of the
  Middle Belt story. *(high for the pairs; repo aliases and wikipedia-southern-kaduna)*

### Colonial policy: Hausa as the language of rule
- **Interpreters and agents.** Hausa interpreters came with the British into Tivland in 1906.
  The victory declaration to the Tiv was written and read in Hausa, then put into Tiv by two
  Hausa Muslims, Mohammed and Maradu. Hausa clerks, messengers, scribes and police
  (*dogarai*) ran tax collection in Idoma Division until the 1930s, when cost-cutting
  replaced them with cheaper Idoma staff. *(high: ochonu-2008)*
- **The administrative model was the emirate.** "Pagan administration" was meant to make
  Middle Belt peoples "more like the Hausa subjects of the defunct Sokoto Caliphate". Hausa
  chiefs were "foisted on the people" in Tiv and Idoma Divisions. *(high: ochonu-2008)*
- **Language infrastructure.** The British introduced the Latin *boko* spelling of Hausa in
  the 1930s. The colonial army ran on Hausa and its pidgin, Barikanchi. *Gaskiya Ta Fi
  Kwabo* (1939) was the first Hausa newspaper and a key tool for reaching the grassroots.
  Through the Northern Region, Hausa was built into "administration, public services, law,
  media and education" (Arasanyin 1995, after Paden 1968).
  *(high: wikipedia-hausa-language, wikipedia-gaskiya, arasanyin-1995)*
- **Conversion as "becoming Hausa".** Harnischfeger (2006): converts in the Middle Belt
  "tend to assume the language, culture and political loyalties" of the Muslim settlers.
  Shobana Shankar: over the decades, Western-educated Christians in the North were
  "subjected to intense pressure from British and Muslim authorities to become Muslims",
  with government jobs as the bait. Northern Christians were branded *Kafirai*,
  "southerners at heart", and treated as traitors to the Northern system.
  *(high: harnischfeger-2006 via search; shankar-2014; enwerem-1995)*

### The cost: languages lost
- Hausa "has replaced many other languages especially in the north-central and north-eastern
  part of Nigeria" (Wikipedia, Hausa). The last speaker of Gamo-Ningi died in the 1980s;
  Goemai is threatened, with children learning Hausa first. *(high)*
- Igboanusi and Peter (2004): "The dream of 'one north' makes Hausa a lingua franca in
  northern Nigeria, with the potential of annihilating the over 200 indigenous languages
  spoken in that region." *(high: igboanusi-peter-2004)*

### The pushback
- Arasanyin (1995) found that Middle Belt minorities prefer English to Hausa, and links this
  to "the historical imposition of Hausa", which left "low social tolerance for the
  language". Since state creation, the groups "have responded in fashions that affirm their
  individual identities rather than compromise them for the sake of preservation of the
  traditional political coalescence under the Hausa lingua franca". *(high: arasanyin-1995)*
- Missions wrote local languages down (the Gospel of Mark in Wukari Jukun, 1914). Cultural
  festivals were revived or founded, for example Nzem Berom in 1980–81.
  *(high: wikipedia-wukari, wikipedia-berom)*

### The nuance
Across much of the belt, Hausa is what lets its many peoples talk to each other: the
Plateau, Nasarawa, Niger, the FCT, Southern Kaduna, Taraba, Adamawa and southern Bauchi.
Arasanyin says that "decades before policy intervention, Hausa was well established as the
lingua franca of the Middle Belt and a social stabilizer". **That is not true of Benue or of
most of Kogi.** There, Tiv, Idoma, Igede, Igala, Ebira and Okun Yoruba are the community
languages, English carries public life, and Hausa is mainly the language of Hausa settler
quarters and markets (section 2). Speaking Hausa does not make anyone Hausa, and
being Hausa does not mean being Muslim (the Maguzawa are non-Muslim Hausa). What Middle
Belters rejected was Hausa *as a ruling identity*, not Hausa as a market language.

## 8. Northernization and the Sardauna's "One North", 1951–1966

### Northernization: what it was
- It was the Northern Region government's policy, under Premier Ahmadu Bello (1954–1966).
  Its aim, in Enwerem's words, was "to ensure that indigenes of the Northern Region are
  appointed to all types of posts in the public service" at both regional and federal level.
  *(high: enwerem-1995 ch. 2 fn. 68)*
- Bello's own summary in a press interview: "the northerner first. If we can't get a
  northerner, then we take an expatriate." A policy statement of April 1960 set out to
  Northernize the regional service and to secure northern posts in the federal service and
  corporations. *(medium: secondary sources quoting the interview and the statement)*
- **Why it was adopted.** After the service was regionalised in 1954, few northerners had
  the Western education to compete; southerners dominated the colonial service and the
  railway. In a 1952 debate a northern ruler likened Nigeria to a compound in which "Mr.
  West's and Mr. East's houses have already gone far on the way".
  *(high: wikipedia-ahmadu-bello, wikipedia-nigerianisation)*
- **Effect.** Southern recruitment into the Northern service was limited and northerners were
  pushed up the ranks. Southerners already lived in segregated *Sabon Gari* quarters.
  *(high for the policy; medium for the scale of dismissals: no firm figures found)*

### Middle Belters and Northernization: not the problem
The mission schools had made Middle Belt Christians some of the best-educated people in the
North, and Northernization counted them as northerners. Enwerem's verdict is that Middle Belt
hostility was aimed at the Sardauna's repressive measures, "not at his Northernization
Policy". **The quarrel with "Arewa" was over rule, religion and dignity, not over northern
jobs.** *(high: enwerem-1995 ch. 2 ¶50)*

### "One North, One People" and the conversion drive
- **Symbolism.** Northern self-government was inaugurated at Sokoto, the caliphate's capital,
  not at Kaduna, the regional capital (Enwerem). The date is usually given as 15 March 1959;
  some sources say 1957 or 1958. *(high for Sokoto; date disputed)*
- **The aim.** Enwerem: "At the regional level, Ahmadu Bello's primary interest was to unify
  the North under Islamic religious leadership." And: "Bello's specific target for the final
  unification of the North was to convert the 'pagan' enclaves in the region to Islam." The
  reason was that those enclaves were where Christianity was growing, from 2.7% of the
  North in 1952 to 9.7% in 1963 (Crampton's analysis of the census). *(high: enwerem-1995
  ch. 2 ¶19–20)*
- **The means.** Jama'atu Nasril Islam was founded in 1962 after Bello's pilgrimage, together
  with a Council of Mallams. Membership was Muslim-only, yet these bodies were presented as
  speaking for all northerners. A former official recalled government planes and vehicles
  being used for the campaigns. In 1964 Bello told the World Muslim League: "I hope when we
  clean Nigeria we will go further afield in Africa." *(high for JNI and the speech; medium
  for the use of state resources)*
- **The numbers.** Ojukwu later said, dismissively: "We did not go to war because the
  Sardauna came down to Tiv land and converted fifty thousand." No independent count was
  found, and some converts returned to their old faith after 1966. *(disputed)*
- **Co-option.** Under his "consensus approach" Bello brought Christians into the NPC and
  government. Among them was the Rev. David Lot, founder of the Middle Zone League, who was
  co-opted with government resources (Enwerem ¶69). *(high)*
- **Repression.** UMBC supporters faced the Native Authority police and the *alkali* courts.
  The Tiv were punished collectively in 1960 and 1964, and Tarka was detained in 1961 and
  tried in 1962 (section 9). *(high)*
- **The North by religion in 1958.** The minorities commission counted about 11 million
  Muslims, 4.5 million "pagans" and 550,000 Christians in the North. The "pagans" were the
  people both sides were competing for. *(high: shankar-2014)*

### 1966: the break, and a bargain
- Enwerem: the "indigenous non-Muslim Northerners, especially the Christians of the Middle
  Belt", "hailed" the January 1966 coup, which killed the Sardauna, as freedom from an
  Islamic threat. But they did not want to trade religious domination for ethnic domination.
  *(high: enwerem-1995 ch. 2 ¶36)*
- Then northern leaders courted them. Tarka met the Makaman Bida and Aminu Kano, and they
  "reached an agreement to work for a united North, and northern interests". That pact
  underlay Gowon's rise and the North's acceptance of state creation (Enwerem ¶50). **The
  Middle Belt sided with "the North" in 1966–67 on terms: that the Northern Region be broken
  into states.** *(high)*

## 10. State creation: how the Middle Belt was carved

### Why it mattered
The Northern Region held about three-quarters of Nigeria's land and a majority of the seats
in the federal House: 174 of 312 in 1959. As long as it stayed one unit, the NPC could rule
it and weigh heavily on the federation. A separate Middle Belt region would have broken
that, which is why the NPC opposed it and the Action Group backed it. For Middle Belt
peoples, a state of their own meant their own governor, civil service, schools, budget and
chiefs, outside the emirate system. *(high for seats; medium for the motive, which is analysis)*

### Before 1967
- **The colonial provinces split the Middle Belt.** Munshi Province (renamed Benue in 1918),
  Plateau (carved out of Bauchi in 1926), Kabba, Ilorin, Niger, Nassarawa, the Southern Zaria
  districts of Zaria Province, and the southern districts of Bauchi and Adamawa. Several
  were run from emirate capitals. *(high)*
- **1957–58:** the Willink Commission refused a Middle Belt region (section 9).
- **1963:** the Mid-West Region was carved out of the West, the only new region of the First
  Republic. The Middle Belt got nothing. Zamani Lekwot later blamed politicians who saw it as
  a threat. *(high)*

- **They gave homelands.** Benue to the Tiv, Idoma and Igede; Plateau to the Berom, Ngas and

  chiefdoms. Minority Rights Group says the 1976 split of Benue State "gave the Tiv a
  homeland". *(high)*

  states. **Southern Kaduna, southern Bauchi, Zuru, southern Gombe and southern Borno** remain
  minorities inside states with emirate majorities. That is where the sharpest conflicts

  emirate towns in Nasarawa, and the Igala as a minority in Kogi's politics. *(medium)*

## 11. Why they rejected the Arewa idea

"Arewa", as the NPC and its heirs used it, meant one North, led by the heirs of the
caliphate and held together by Islam and the Hausa language. Middle Belt peoples rejected
that idea for reasons that build on each other.

1. **Memory.** The caliphate whose heritage Arewa claims was, for them, the slave raider
   and the army they hid from in the hills (section 5). Barth's 200 boys marched to Sokoto
   and Kwassau's war on the Atyap are their history.
2. **Colonial subordination.** "The North" first reached most of them as rule by emirs and
   imported Hausa chiefs, clerks and tax collectors, under a British system that called them
   "pagans" needing "tutelage" (section 6). Their earliest experience of being northern was
   being ruled by other northerners.
3. **Hausanization.** Belonging to the North seemed to require becoming Hausa in language,
   names, dress and faith (section 7). Their languages were dying. Their Christians were
   called *Kafirai* and "southerners at heart".
4. **Religion.** The Sardauna's stated aim of unifying the North "under Islamic religious
   leadership", and of converting the "pagan" enclaves, aimed directly at them. JNI was
   Muslim-only but spoke for all (section 8). Later rounds deepened the fear: the Middle
   Belt led the opposition to a Federal Sharia Court of Appeal in the 1977 Constituent
   Assembly (Enwerem ¶64), and in 2000 twelve northern states adopted sharia. Those included
   **Niger** (North Central), Bauchi, Gombe and Kaduna, where the 2000 riots killed
   thousands. *(high)*
5. **Repression of their politics.** UMBC voters were punished, Tiv villages were burned in
   1960 and 1964, and Tarka was detained and tried (section 9).
6. **Second-class status.** Pogu Bitrus in 2024: some people in the North are treated as
   "more north than others". In 2001 Middle Belt leaders turned down the ACF's invitation
   for fear of being marginalised in it. In 2021 the MBF spoke of "the unhappy marriage for
   the convenience of the Arewa".
7. **Land, chieftaincy and security today.** There are disputes over who is "indigene" in
   Jos, over district heads and emirate authority (Southern Kaduna until 1995–2001, the
   Sayawa until 2024), and over farmer–herder killings. The MBF accuses northern leaders of
   indifference to these killings (sections 10 and 12).

### What they did *not* reject
- **Northern jobs and Northernization.** Middle Belters benefited as northerners (section 8).
- **Hausa as a common language**, where it is one (the Plateau, Nasarawa, Niger, Southern
  Kaduna, Taraba). In Benue and most of Kogi it never was, so there was nothing to keep or
  reject (section 2).
- **The North as a place.** The MBF says it is not separatist and believes in "the oneness"
  of Nigeria.
- **Northern unity on terms.** In 1966 Tarka agreed to "work for a united North" in exchange
  for states. Gowon later chaired the ACF's board of patrons, and Tarka joined the NPN in
  1978–79.

So what they rejected was the Arewa idea as a *ruling identity*: the North as the caliphate's
heir, with Islam and Hausa as the price of full membership. The northern map, the language
and the jobs were never the objection. Muslim Middle Belt peoples (the Ebira, Alago, many
Gbagyi) and the emirate towns inside the belt stand in different places on this, and the
MBF insists that Muslims belong to the Middle Belt too.

  (Lelna), Tiv, Idoma, Gbagyi, Berom, Ngas and Eggon. *(high: leadership-pogu-bitrus)*
  Note that his list includes Nupe, which was a caliphate emirate after the 1830s.

## 13. People by people

Each entry gives the key history, the people's relationship to the caliphate, and its weight
in the "not Arewa" case. "Repo" means the history is already sourced in HistoNaija's data.

### Benue
- **Tiv.** One of Nigeria's largest peoples, over 8 million. They had no king: clans and
  kindreds were led by elders, the *orya*. By tradition they settled at Swem around 1600,
  then spread into the Benue lowlands. Never conquered; they allied with the Jukun against
  the jihad. Last major area conquered by Britain (1906–1911). Placed under a Nupe-born
  Sarkin Makurdi (1914–1945). Makurdi riots 1947. Tor Tiv from 1946. Backbone of the UMBC;
  risings in 1960 and 1964. "Munchi"/"Mitshi", the Hausa name for them, is offensive. *(repo;
  ochonu-2008)*
- **Idoma.** Self-governing clans with elders, titled councils and an *oche*. The Apa
  (Kwararafa) dispersal; time at Idah with the Igala; tribute to the Attah in the 1700s. Doma
  and Bagaji fell to Keffi and Zazzau. The Anglo-Idoma wars ran from 1899 to the 1920s,
  largely against Hausa agents. Och'Idoma from 1947. *(repo; ochonu-2008)*
- **Igede.** The Ogbuloko uprising (1926–29) against "Hausa" clerks and tax. Brought under
  control only in 1922. *(repo; ochonu-2008)*

### Kogi
- **Igala.** A centralised kingdom under the Attah at Idah. Kwararafa heritage through
  Abutu-Eje. The Benin war of 1515–16. Never part of the caliphate; Koton Karfe held. Lost
  the north to Nupe raids in 1853. The British bombarded Idah in 1896 and took Dekina (1903)
  and Ankpa (1906). *(repo)*
- **Ebira.** Elder-run communities, with the Opete stool from Kwararafa tradition. Held off
  Bida and Ilorin under Achigidi Okino (1865–80). Brought under British control in 1903.
  Largely Muslim today, which makes them a key case of a Muslim people that was never a
  caliphate subject. *(repo)*
- **Okun Yoruba** (Owe, Iyagba, Bunu, Ijumu, Oworo). Conquered by Bida from the 1850s;
  forced to give boys and girls as tribute in the 1890s; rose in the Ogidi alliance of 1894.
  Culturally closer to the Ekiti; many reject the Middle Belt label. Sunday Awoniyi, an Okun
  Christian, chaired the ACF's board of trustees (2000–2007). *(repo; wikipedia-acf)*
- **Bassa-Nge, Bassa-Komo, Kakanda, Oworo.** Refugees from the jihad at the confluence;
  Lokoja was a refuge town. *(repo)*

### Plateau
- **Berom.** Autonomous groups with no paramount ruler until the Gbong Gwom Jos (1935, with
  the groups merged under it in 1952). Never conquered. They lost land to tin mining and
  colonial Jos. *(repo)*
- **Ngas (Angas).** By tradition they came from Bornu and settled at Pankshin, Ampang, Amper
  and Kabwir. The hills sheltered them from Bauchi's raids. Gowon, Dimka and Lekwot are
  Ngas. *(repo; wikipedia-yakubu-gowon)*
- **Mwaghavul, Afizere, Anaguta, Irigwe, Tarok, Goemai.** Hill and plateau-edge peoples.
  The Afizere Agwom was both chief and chief priest. The Tarok are an amalgam of several
  peoples. A Muslim Goemai leader near Shendam has spoken for Middle Belt
  self-determination, a reminder that the identity is not only Christian. The Irigwe were
  middlemen who sold captured raiders as slaves. *(repo; intervention-ng-2022)*

### Nasarawa
- **Eggon.** A decentralised hill people in three clans (Anzo, Eholo, Anro); the Hausa called
  them "Madan Dutse". *(repo)*
- **Alago.** An Idomoid people of the Keana salt and Doma. Families commonly include
  Christians, Muslims and traditionalists. Lafia is a Kanuri-ruled emirate in their country.
  *(wikipedia-alago-language; wikipedia-lafia)*
- **Mada, Migili (Koro), Afo, Gwandara.** Keffi (Fulani, about 1802) and Nasarawa (1838) were
  emirates planted among them. Keffi paid Zaria an annual tribute of slaves.
  *(wikipedia-keffi)*
- **Gwandara.** Speak Hausa's closest sister language. By tradition they left Kano under Yaji
  (1349–85) or Rumfa (1463–99) rather than convert. Scholars doubt a mass exodus and suggest
  a small group of non-Muslim Hausa became the core of mixed settlements. *(disputed)*

### Southern Kaduna
- **Atyap.** No king; their elders met at Mabatado. Around 1650 they let Hausa and Bornu
  traders settle at Zangon Katab under the *akabe* pact. They refused Zazzau's tribute in the
  1840s. Kwassau's war (1897–1902) killed over 1,000 and impaled the fighter Marok Gandu; the
  flight from it is remembered as *Tyong Kwasau*. Ruled by Hausa officials from 1912; risings
  in 1910 and 1922; the 1992 crisis; chiefdom in 1995. *(repo; wikipedia-southern-kaduna)*
- **Bajju, Ham (Jaba), Adara (Kadara), Gwong, Nikyob, Ninzo, Agworok, Takad.** Some were not
  subdued by the British until 1915. Nok itself is a Ham village. The Adara were among those
  Lugard named as "never conquered". *(high)*

### FCT and Niger
- **Gbagyi.** Self-governing settlements under an *osu*. Raided by Zazzau, Bida, Kontagora
  and Abuja. Put under the Zaria, Abuja and Bida emirates by the British. Displaced from
  their ancestral land (including Zuma Rock) for the new capital in 1976. Islam came with the
  jihad, but most conversions happened in colonial and later times; most Gbagyi are
  Christian. *(repo; wikipedia-gbagyi)*
- **Koro.** Abuja (Suleja) was founded on Koro land by the Hausa exiles. *(high)*
- **Kambari, Dakarkari (Lelna), Dukawa.** Conquered by Kontagora (1858–64) or held out in the
  Zuru hills, which the British made an "emirate". The Dakarkari are well known in the army.
  *(high: wikipedia-kontagora, wikipedia-dakarkari)*
- **Nupe.** Old kingdom, Muslim emirate since the 19th century, mostly Muslim today. See
  section 14. *(repo)*
- **Busa and Bariba (Borgu).** Never in the caliphate; Kisra kingship; split between Britain
  and France in 1898. *(repo)*

### Taraba and Adamawa
- **Jukun.** Heirs of Kwararafa under the Aku Uka of Wukari. The Aku Uka sat in the Northern
  executive council in 1958 alongside the Sultan and the emirs of Kano and Katsina. Many
  are Christian; the present Aku Uka worships in a palace chapel. *(wikipedia-jukun,
  wikipedia-wukari)*
- **Kuteb.** A federation of clans under a priest-king, the Kwe Kukwen. Gomwalk's 1975 change
  to the Takum stool led to conflict with the Jukun in 1991. *(wikipedia-kuteb)*
- **Chamba, Mumuye, Mambila.** Chamba warbands broke Kwararafa; Muri (1817) raided the
  Mumuye hills. *(repo)*
- **Bachama and Bata, Kamwe, Kilba.** The Bachama and Bata were protected by dan Fodio's own
  instruction; the Kamwe and Kilba held their mountain kingdoms. *(repo)*

### Southern Bauchi, Gombe and Borno
- **Sayawa (Zaar).** Under the Bauchi emirate through colonial rule; risings in 1991 and
  1995; chiefdom created in December 2024 and contested. *(high)*
- **Tangale and Waja.** The Mai Tangle of Billiri, a line dated to 1295 by tradition; held
  the hills. *(repo)*
- **Bura, Pabir and Chibok.** Biu fought off the Gombe Fulani. Pogu Bitrus of the MBF is
  from Chibok. *(repo; leadership-pogu-bitrus)*

## 14. Where the "not Arewa" claim is strong, and where it is weak

### Strong
- **Benue and most of Kogi pass both tests** (section 2). They were never under Sokoto, and
  Hausa is not their language. Their only "northern" link before 1900 was trade and raiding,
  except for the Okun west of Kogi, which was under Bida until 1897.
- Very well documented for the Plateau, the Tiv, the Jukun of Wukari, the Igala, the Idoma
  heartland, the Ebira hills, Borgu, the Bachama and the hill peoples of Adamawa and Gombe.
  None was ever ruled by Sokoto or Bornu.
- The colonial imposition of emirs and Hausa agents on peoples who had never been under them
  is documented from British records themselves (Ochonu; the Zaria Residents' reports;
  Lugard's own words).
- The organised political demand is continuous from 1949 to today.

### Weak or complicated
- **Emirates were inside the belt.** Keffi, Nasarawa, Lafia, Jema'a, Wase, Muri, Bida, Agaie,
  Lapai, Kontagora, Ilorin, Pategi and Lafiagi. Their people are Middle Belt by geography and
  Arewa by heritage.
- **Nupe and Ilorin are caliphate heirs.** The Etsu Nupe's line is partly Fulani. Kperogi
  argues that Niger State was largely in Sokoto Province and is "culturally
  indistinguishable" from the far north.
- **Kwara and Kogi Yoruba** look to the Yoruba south-west, not the Middle Belt.
- **Muslims of the Middle Belt.** Perhaps half the region is Muslim: the Ebira, many Gbagyi,
  Alago, Nupe and Igala. "Middle Belt = Christian" erases them, even though MBF leaders
  insist Muslims belong.
- **Middle Belt elites also joined Arewa bodies.** Gowon chaired the ACF's board of patrons
  (2000); Awoniyi chaired its trustees; the North Central states sit in the Northern
  Governors' Forum.
- **Hausa is the lingua franca** across the northern and central parts of the belt (Plateau,
  Nasarawa, Niger, FCT, Southern Kaduna, Taraba), a legacy partly of the colonial policy
  described in sections 6 and 7. This point does **not** apply to Benue or most of Kogi.
- **The violence ran both ways** (Yelwa 2004, and reprisals in Plateau and Southern Kaduna).
- **The claims to check carefully:**
  - *"Bauchi means land of slaves."* This is **not supported**. Tradition names Bauchi after a
    hunter, Baushe.
  - *"The Middle Belt was never conquered."* True of many peoples, false of the Nupe, Okun,
    Kambari, Ilorin and Doma.
  - *"Slave raiding depopulated the Middle Belt."* True only locally (Mason 1969).
  - *The Ibrahim Nagwamatse "slave in my mouth" quote:* no source found.

## 16. Open questions and unverified points

- The UMBC's founding date: 1955 or 1957?
- Willink's exact wording on the Middle Belt (the report itself is on Scribd, unread).
- The death tolls of the 1960 and 1964 Tiv riots (from 16 to over 4,000).
- Exact mission dates: SUM's first station at Wase in 1904; the Dutch Reformed mission among
  the Tiv in 1911.
- Whether the Sardauna's conversion tours of 1964–65 reached Middle Belt areas, and in what
  numbers. No source found.
- The Middle Belt's share of the army's rank and file in the 1960s (Luckham 1971, tables
  unread).
- Kafanchan 1987: casualty and spread figures not checked.
- How widely Hausa is really spoken, state by state. No survey was found; section 2's
  language columns rest on broadcasting, schooling and the literature. A sociolinguistic
  survey of Makurdi, Lokoja, Idah and Okene would settle it for Benue and Kogi.
- The Sardauna's conversion drive: tour routes and real numbers. The "fifty thousand in Tiv
  land" figure is only Ojukwu's remark.
- Northernization: how many southern staff were actually dismissed or kept out, and when.
  The press quote "the northerner first" is undated.
- The date of Northern self-government: 15 March 1959 is the usual date; Wikipedia says 1957
  and Enwerem's index 1958.
- Whether Tarka or other Middle Belt leaders lobbied Gowon directly for Decree No. 14, beyond
  Enwerem's account of the Tarka–Makaman Bida–Aminu Kano pact.
